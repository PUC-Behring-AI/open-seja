What this page is

Everything below is drawn from SEJA's own record of its uncertainty — the unresolved tensions logged when the documentation architecture was chosen, the "what I don't know" questions in the extended metacommunication template, and the ethical scenarios and post-deployment questions in the as-coded metacommunication section. Nothing here is a new claim about the harness. It is a synthesis of claims the harness already makes about itself.

That provenance is the point. A system whose stated quality principle is that it must surface what is not yet known, not only what has been decided owes its readers a page where those unknowns are collected rather than distributed across artifacts only its author reads. This is that page, and its existence is a test the harness can fail: if the uncertainties recorded elsewhere have grown and this page has not, the epistemic posture has become decorative.

The accountability limit

The sharpest open question is one the harness answers plainly and unflatteringly. SEJA's governance model makes the designer's intent explicit and traceable, which supports accountability when AI-assisted software causes harm. But SEJA cannot enforce that the designer's intent is itself ethical. It can only enforce that the implementation matches the intent. A designer who writes an unethical metacommunication message gets a system that faithfully executes it.

The harness evaluated itself against three ethical scenarios, and the third is the one with no mechanism behind it. A designer who treats confirmation gates as rubber-stamps — approving proposed content without reading it — defeats the model entirely, and the recorded answer is that SEJA has no recourse: the designer's epistemic responsibility is not enforceable by tooling. SEJA can make the responsibility visible; it cannot make it felt.

This bounds every claim the rest of the documentation makes. Governance here means traceability of authorship, not guarantee of judgment. Read against a literature that sometimes treats process compliance as a proxy for design quality, SEJA is unusually explicit that the two come apart — but being explicit about a gap is not the same as closing it.

The false-completeness risk

The second recorded ethical question turns the epistemic posture against itself. A harness that surfaces gaps and unknowns may produce exactly the confidence it was built to prevent: "I have addressed all the gaps SEJA found, so I must be done." The record is blunt about why that inference does not hold — gap detection is heuristic and bounded by what is visible in the artifacts, and there will always be gaps SEJA cannot detect.

The evaluation scenario for this is concrete: the harness reports full coverage of designed features while missing a connection between a problem scenario and a feature's metacommunication intention. The question the scenario asks is not whether the miss is possible — it is whether the heuristic label on the report makes its own confidence legible to a reader who wants reassurance. That is a communicability question, and it is open.

For a scholar the interesting move here is structural. Confidence labeling is SEJA's attempt to make a tool's epistemic limits part of its metacommunication rather than a footnote to it. Whether a label changes what a motivated reader concludes is an empirical question no one has answered for this system.

What the harness does not know about its readers

The metacommunication template requires the designer to state what they do not know about the people they are designing for, and SEJA's own answers are substantial. It does not know how much formal semiotic engineering training its user has, whether they work alone or in a team, what domain their project addresses, how long they have used the harness, or — most pointedly — whether they have hit its limits in ways they have not reported.

The gap widens for affected others. The full range of organizational contexts is unknown; so is the extent to which team members who are not themselves harness practitioners are affected by decisions made under its governance; so is the space of misuse scenarios in which the governance model is exploited or circumvented rather than followed.

Three misuses are anticipated and named: producing design artifacts that look authoritative but were authored without designer confirmation; using the governance vocabulary to create an appearance of design rigour without the substance; and bypassing the commit-time gate to suppress leak detection. Anticipating a misuse is not preventing it, and two of the three are behavioural rather than technical.

Where the harness may over- or under-engineer

Two watch areas are recorded as open, and they pull in opposite directions.

Over-engineering. The anticipated negative effect of the design is increased ceremony for simple tasks — the full lifecycle overhead applied to a one-line change. The corresponding ethical watch area names the same thing more sharply: the risk of over-engineering design artefacts for small projects where the ceremony exceeds the value. Neither has been resolved by evidence; both are held as things to watch.

Accountability theatre. The other watch area is the risk of using the governance vocabulary to perform accountability without practising it. This is the misuse scenario restated as a systemic property rather than an individual failing, and it is the point at which SEJA's heaviest mechanisms — audit trails, decision records, confirmation gates — become indistinguishable from their own imitation.

A reader deciding whether the harness over- or under-engineers reflection should notice that the record does not claim to know. It names both failure modes, provides mechanisms aimed at each, and leaves the calibration question open.

What has not been evaluated

The post-deployment section of the metacommunication record is, by its own account, largely unpopulated — and that is the most consequential uncertainty on this page.

Vision versus actual use
Unevaluated in a multi-user context. The harness has been used primarily by a single designer — its own author — who has deep knowledge of its design intent. Every claim about how the harness communicates its intent to a user rests on a sample that includes the person who wrote the intent.
Unanticipated uses
To be populated from field observation. One near-anticipated use is recorded: the harness as a pedagogical instrument in a semiotic engineering course.
Effects in use
One anticipated positive effect (reduced drift from design intent) and one anticipated negative effect (ceremony overhead) are named. Unanticipated effects are recorded as unknown.
Issues warranting redesign
To be evaluated as the harness matures, with the two watch areas above as the standing agenda.

For a scholar this is the load-bearing caveat. The harness is a designed artifact with an articulated theory and an unusually complete record of its own intent — and almost no evidence about its reception. Read it as a design proposition, not a validated result.

Tensions in the documentation itself

The collection you are reading was chosen over two alternatives, and the research that chose it logged four tensions as unresolved rather than settled. Three of them are visible from this page.

No single organizing axis satisfies everyone
Documentation type, system layer, and reader path each serve some audiences at the cost of others. Routing by reader path — the choice made here — is a bet, not a solution.
Theory prominence cuts both ways
The semiotic-engineering grounding is the harness's intellectual differentiator and simultaneously an adoption barrier. This track exists because the tension was resolved by separating the audiences rather than by deciding how much theory a general reader should meet.
Comprehensiveness costs sustainability
More documentation means more surface that can go stale. This is the cost accepted when the collection grew, and it falls hardest on pages describing internals that change.
Routing risks duplication
Audience-routing serves readers best but invites content to be restated per track; single-sourcing prevents that but demands aggressive cross-referencing. The discipline that tracks recommend an order and never restate content is the answer to this tension — and it is a discipline, which means it can lapse.
