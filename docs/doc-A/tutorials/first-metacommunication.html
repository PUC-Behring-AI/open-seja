Writing Your First Metacommunication Message

In Chapter 1, you answered a question about your design intent using "I" and "you" phrasing. That answer became your metacommunication message -- a first-person statement from you (the designer) to your user about what you built and why. This tutorial explores what that message is, why it matters, and how to write a good one.

Prerequisites

  • A working SEJA project (complete Chapter 1 first)
  • The to-do app project (my-todo-app) open in your terminal

What Is a Metacommunication Message?

A metacommunication message is communication about communication. When you design software, you are sending a message to your users through the interface. The metacommunication message makes that message explicit: it is a first-person statement where you, the designer, tell the user what you intended them to understand and experience.

In semiotic engineering -- the theoretical foundation behind SEJA -- every piece of software is a one-shot message from designer to user, delivered through the interface. The designer is not present when the user interacts with the software. The interface must communicate on the designer's behalf. The metacommunication message captures what you want that communication to say.

What just happened?

You have just encountered the core idea behind SEJA's design layer. Most agent harnesses track what the code does. SEJA also tracks what the designer intends the code to communicate. This is not just a philosophical distinction -- it changes how the agent plans, implements, and reviews your code. When the agent reads your metacommunication message, it understands not just the feature requirements but the user experience you are trying to create. For a deeper exploration, see The Semiotic Engineering Foundation.

The I/You Phrasing Convention

SEJA's metacommunication messages follow a strict phrasing convention: "I" is the designer, "you" is the user. This is not arbitrary -- it forces you to think from two perspectives simultaneously: what you designed and what the user experiences.

Before and after comparison

Consider how the same design intent reads in third person versus first person:

Third-person (typical spec language)

The application shall display tasks sorted by priority. Users can filter tasks by status. The system supports three priority levels.

First-person metacommunication (SEJA convention)

When you open the task list, I show your tasks sorted by priority because I want you to see the most urgent items first. I have given you three priority levels -- high, medium, and low -- because I believe more granularity would slow you down without helping you decide. I let you filter by status so you can focus on what is actionable right now.

The first-person version captures three things the third-person version misses:

  • Intent: why tasks are sorted by priority (to surface urgency)
  • Trade-off: why only three levels (more granularity would slow users down)
  • User benefit: what the user gains from the filter (focus on actionable items)

Writing Your Message

Open the design skill to update your metacommunication message: 

/design update metacomm

SEJA jumps directly to the metacommunication section. You will see your current message and an invitation to revise it.

Step 1: Start with what the user sees

Begin your message by describing the first thing the user encounters. Use "When you..." to anchor the statement in the user's experience: 

"When you open my to-do app, I show you a clean list of your
tasks, sorted by priority. I want you to know at a glance
what needs your attention first."

Step 2: Explain your design decisions

For each major decision, state what you chose and why. Use "I have designed..." or "I chose..." to make the decision explicit: 

"I have designed the priority system with only three levels
-- high, medium, and low -- because I believe that finer
granularity creates decision fatigue without improving
your ability to prioritize."

Step 3: Address what you chose not to do

The most revealing part of a metacommunication message is what you decided against. State the alternative and why you rejected it: 

"I chose not to support due-date-based sorting by default,
because I want you to think about importance, not just
urgency. You can still see due dates, but priority comes
first."

Expected output

After you confirm your revised message, SEJA updates section 11 of product-design/product-design-as-intended.md with your new metacommunication text. You can verify by opening the file and looking for the metacommunication section.

What just happened?

You just wrote a metacommunication message that captures not just what your to-do app does, but what you intend it to say to the user through its interface. The "I/you" convention is not just a writing style -- it is a cognitive tool. By forcing you to speak as the designer addressing the user, it surfaces assumptions, trade-offs, and values that third-person specs leave implicit.

This message is stored in product-design/product-design-as-intended.md and is read by the agent at the start of every planning session. When the agent plans a new feature, it checks the feature against your metacommunication message to ensure consistency. When the agent reviews code, it evaluates whether the implementation communicates what you intended.

The theoretical basis for this approach comes from Clarisse de Souza's semiotic engineering, where software is understood as a designed message from the designer to the user. For the full theoretical background, see The Semiotic Engineering Foundation.

Common Mistakes

When writing metacommunication messages, avoid these patterns:

Writing a feature list instead of a message
Avoid: "The app has tasks, tags, priorities, and filters."
Instead: "I give you tasks you can tag and prioritize because I want you to organize your work the way that makes sense to you."
Forgetting the "why"
Avoid: "When you open the app, I show you your tasks."
Instead: "When you open the app, I show you your tasks sorted by priority because I want the most urgent items to catch your eye immediately."
Using third-person language
Avoid: "Users can create tasks with priorities."
Instead: "I let you create tasks with priorities so you can decide what matters most."
