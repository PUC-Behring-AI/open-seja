Reflective Practice in SEJA

Semiotic engineering is one of the two traditions SEJA sits inside. The other is Donald Schon's reflective practice (Schon 1983, The Reflective Practitioner). The connection is not incidental: de Souza adopted Schon's stance as the foundational epistemology of semiotic engineering (de Souza 2005, Ch. 1), explicitly rejecting the technical-rationality view in which design is the straightforward application of theory to practice. If design is instead a reflective conversation with the materials of a situation, then a tool that governs AI-assisted design has to make room for that conversation. This chapter explains Schon's three registers of reflection, takes two serious counter-readings seriously, and shows exactly which SEJA mechanism carries each register.

Schon's Three Registers

Schon distinguishes three registers of reflection, and it is worth keeping them separate because each licenses a different kind of move.

Reflection-in-action
Thinking about what you are doing while you are doing it -- the in-the-moment pause to second-guess a step before you commit to it. This is the register of the skilled practitioner who feels something is off mid-stroke and adjusts. It happens inside the action, not after it, and its hallmark is the productive hesitation: the beat where you stop to consider before proceeding.
Reflection-on-action
Pausing after the work is done to think back over what happened. This is the retrospective register: what did I do, what worked, what surprised me, what would I do differently. It operates on a completed piece of work and turns it into a lesson.
Reflection-on-practice
The longest-horizon register, where you step back from any single piece of work to surface and criticize the tacit patterns you have been running without noticing -- the habits of your practice itself. This is not about one artifact; it is about the recurring shape of how you work across many artifacts and sessions.

The three registers are nested by time horizon: in-action lives inside the moment, on-action lives just after the moment, and on-practice lives across many moments. A tool that wants to support reflective practice cannot collapse them into one "reflection feature"; it needs a distinct surface at each horizon. SEJA has exactly three, one per register, which the mapping section below lays out.

Abduction: The Reasoning Mode Reflection Runs On

Schon's account pairs naturally with Peirce's abduction. Abduction is the mode of reasoning in which you observe a surprising fact, entertain a hypothesis that would explain it if true, and provisionally hold that hypothesis as your best available reading until something contradicts it. Peirce is blunt about the provisionality: an abductive conclusion holds only until better evidence arrives. It is the only mode of reasoning that admits genuinely creative formulations, and it is the engine of reflective sense-making -- the reflective practitioner is, in Peircean terms, running abduction on their own work.

This is why SEJA's design documents, and the foundations material they draw on, are written in a consistently provisional voice -- "we currently read," "this is our current best reading" -- rather than in the voice of settled fact. The provisionality is not hedging for its own sake; it is the grammatical form that keeps the harness's self-understanding abductive. SEJA operationalizes abduction most visibly in the council-debate mode of /research --deep, where a small group of named experts each run their own abductive reading of the same question and are brought into structured disagreement, so that the synthesis handed back carries its counter-readings on its face rather than pretending a single recommendation is the only plausible one.

Mapping Registers to Mechanisms

SEJA carries one concrete scaffold per register. Each is deliberately minimal -- the aim is to place a small structured pause at each horizon, not to build a reflection ceremony a designer will learn to skip.

Schon register SEJA mechanism What the pause does
Reflection-in-action Decision-point rationale at every AskUserQuestion Turns a silent branch into a small visible deliberation before you commit
Reflection-on-action Post-skill QA logging and the post-action reflection note Records what happened, what deviated, and what is now less certain
Reflection-on-practice The /reflect skill, product and practice lenses Surfaces recurring patterns across sessions in your own words

Reflection-in-action: decision-point rationale

Whenever a SEJA skill pauses to ask you a question -- pick a profile, confirm a marker flip, choose the next step -- it carries a short rationale for each option: not just what the option is but why it is on the menu and when it is or is not recommended. This is the smallest reflection-in-action scaffold the harness can build. It converts a silent branch, which you would otherwise pass through on autopilot, into a tiny deliberation. The convention is spelled out in the harness's constraints reference, and its rationale is explicitly Schonian: the harness is a proposer, the designer is the decider, and no option may be pre-accepted by framing alone. You can read the rationale and choose the other option anyway; the point is that you paused to read it.

Reflection-on-action: post-skill QA logging

When a skill completes, the post-skill hook captures the session's questions and answers -- by default into a companion <prefix><id>-qa-<slug>.md file collocated with the artifact, or, for a handful of skills whose artifact already is a transcript (like /research) or whose Q&A belongs with the plan (/plan, /implement), inline within the artifact itself. The heavier skills also emit a brief reflection-on-action note: what actually happened, what deviated from the plan, and what is now less certain than it was at the start. The note is short on purpose -- it is not a postmortem, it is the smallest possible act of looking back before the next turn begins.

Reflection-on-practice: the /reflect skill

The longest-horizon register needs its own surface, and that surface is the /reflect skill. In its default conversational mode, /reflect anchors a session on artifacts you choose and then asks which lens you are reflecting through. The product lens examines what you built -- a feature, a design decision, a finished spec -- and asks whether it does what you intended; this is reflection-on-action at the level of an artifact. The practice lens examines how you worked -- what patterns emerge, where you hesitated, what you would do differently as a practitioner; this is Schon's reflection-on-practice at the level of a session. Crucially, /reflect records your words verbatim and does not prescribe what to change. For patterns you cannot see inside a single session, --telemetry mines briefs, QA logs, and pending-ledger entries for recurring sequences, duration outliers, and drift, and --deep adds a visualization layer with an event matrix and a transition graph. Those modes are how reflection-on-practice reaches across weeks of work rather than a single turn.

A Counter-Reading: Eraut on Deliberative vs. Reactive Reflection

It would be too easy to present Schon's registers as an unqualified good and SEJA's scaffolds as their obvious operationalization. Michael Eraut (1994, Developing Professional Knowledge and Competence) is sharp about the limits, and taking him seriously sharpens the design. Eraut's broad concern is that "reflection" smuggles together very different cognitive activities under one honorific word. In particular he distinguishes deliberative reflection -- the considered, time-affording kind that Schon's celebrated examples actually depict -- from reactive reflection, the split-second adjustment made under time pressure with no room for genuine deliberation. His worry is that much of what gets called reflection-in-action is really reactive, and that dressing reactive adjustment in the language of deliberation overstates how much considered thought is actually happening.

For SEJA this is a direct challenge to the decision-point rationale scaffold. A designer clicking through an AskUserQuestion under time pressure is closer to Eraut's reactive pole than his deliberative one; the rationale text may scroll past unread. The honest reading is that the scaffold can only afford deliberation, not compel it -- it holds the door open for a considered pause, but a rushed designer will pass through reactively. The design response SEJA takes is to keep the rationale short enough to be read in the reactive mode while still carrying the contextual "recommended when / not recommended when" reasoning that would reward a deliberative reader. That is a compromise, and Eraut would be right to note it is only a partial answer.

A Second Counter-Reading: Boud on Reflection as Genre Performance

David Boud (2013) makes a related argument from the setting of educational reflective writing: when a reflection becomes an assessed artifact, it stops being a thinking tool and starts being a genre performance. The learner discovers the expected shape of a "good reflection" and produces that shape, whether or not the underlying cognitive work happened. The reflection becomes a ritual whose form is rewarded regardless of its substance.

This is the most pointed critique of SEJA's whole reflection apparatus, and it applies squarely. By turning reflection into something the harness asks you to produce on a schedule -- a rationale at every decision point, a note after every heavy skill, a /reflect session on demand -- SEJA risks training a habit of shape without substance: the designer learns to produce reflection-shaped output without doing the reflecting. We find this position instructive even where we do not fully accept it. The version we accept is that the scaffolds must stay small enough that performance is not worth the effort -- if a reflection note is two sentences, there is little to game. The version we do not accept is the implied alternative, that no scaffolding at all produces better reflection; our reading is that an unassisted designer under deadline reflects less, not more honestly. The /reflect skill's verbatim recording is a deliberate hedge against Boud's critique: by not prescribing what a reflection should conclude, it refuses to define the rewarded shape that genre performance would imitate.

What We Are Uncertain About

We are not confident the post-action reflection loop will stay short. The failure mode we most worry about is that it grows into a boilerplate paragraph the designer stops reading -- which, per Boud, would make the scaffold worse than no reflection at all. Keeping it minimal is a design choice that has to be re-defended every time the loop is tempted to grow.

We are also uncertain about the council-debate as an operationalization of abduction. The debate reliably produces plural readings; we are less sure the synthesis it hands back does justice to the disagreement rather than collapsing it, because a single recommendation is easier to act on than a preserved tension. If the synthesis flattens the plurality, the abductive character is nominal. This is the same uncertainty that shadows the "distributed we to collective we to I" mapping in the previous chapter, seen from the reflection side.

What This Means for SEJA Users

The reflection scaffolds are not busywork the harness imposes; they are three small pauses placed at Schon's three horizons so that reflective practice becomes a habit you do not have to remember to adopt. Read the rationale before you click. Read the post-action note before you start the next task. Run /reflect when you want to step back and look at what you have built or how you have been working. And take the counter-readings seriously: if any of these scaffolds starts to feel like a ritual you are performing rather than a pause you are using, that is the signal that it has grown too heavy and should be trimmed. The next chapter draws these threads together into the thesis that unifies the whole harness: that SEJA is built to be governable and epistemic at once.
