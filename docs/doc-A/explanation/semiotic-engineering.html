The Semiotic Engineering Foundation

SEJA is not a neutral productivity wrapper around Claude Code. It is a harness with a theory of what software is, and every mechanism it imposes -- the first-person design specs, the marker system, the lifecycle hooks, the council debates -- follows from that theory. This chapter lays out the theory. It draws on semiotic engineering as Clarisse de Souza articulated it in The Semiotic Engineering of Human-Computer Interaction (de Souza 2005), and it shows, concept by concept, where each idea surfaces in the harness you are using.

You do not need this chapter to operate SEJA; the tutorials and how-to guides carry the critical path. Read this when you find yourself asking why -- why the design intent is written in the first person, why the harness treats a comment marker as a semiotic act, why it insists on surfacing what it does not know. The short answer to all of those questions is the same: SEJA reads software as a message, and it is built to help you send that message deliberately.

Software as Designed Communication

The central premise of semiotic engineering is that software is a message. Not a product, not a tool, not a system in the abstract -- a message that a designer sends to a user, mediated by code that speaks on the designer's behalf at the moment of use. De Souza's formulation (de Souza 2005, Ch. 1 and Ch. 3) is that human-computer interaction is a special case of computer-mediated human communication: the interface is the channel through which the designer talks to the user, and the user talks back, across a distance in time.

This reframing moves responsibility. If the system is a message, then a usability failure is not a mechanical fault in an autonomous artifact -- it is a communication breakdown between two people, one of whom happens to be absent. When a user cannot figure out what to do, the designer has failed to make themselves understood. That is a diagnosable failure, the way communication breakdowns are diagnosable, rather than a mysterious defect in a machine with a will of its own.

For a tool built to sit between a human designer and an AI coding agent, this premise does real work. It refuses the framing in which "the AI decided" is a complete account of anything. Under semiotic engineering, the software the agent helps produce is still your message to your users; the agent is a medium through which you compose it, not an independent author of it. Everything else in SEJA follows from holding that line.

The Designer's Deputy

De Souza gives the message a speaker. She calls it the designer's deputy: the stand-in that speaks the designer's intentions to the user at run time, when the designer is not in the room. The deputy is not the system itself and it is not an autonomous agent with its own goals; it is the designer's voice speaking through the system. Every label, every default value, every error message, every state transition is a line of the deputy's script. When the user clicks around an application, they are reading that script. When the script fails them, the designer failed them through the deputy.

The deputy concept is why SEJA is so insistent about authorship. In an AI-assisted pipeline, there is a genuine risk that the deputy stops speaking for the designer and starts speaking for the model -- that the agent, drafting labels and copy and design decisions at speed, quietly becomes the author of the message rather than its scribe. SEJA's entire governance apparatus exists to keep the deputy accountable to a human. The design intent is human-authored; the agent may record lifecycle state but not rewrite intent; consequential moves pause for human confirmation. Each of those rules is a way of keeping your hand on the deputy's script.

The Metacommunication Template

If software is a message, the message has a shape. De Souza proposed a template for it (de Souza 2005, Ch. 1, p. 25 and Ch. 3, p. 84) with three structural parts: what the designer believes about the user (their profile, goals, and context); what the designer built (the system description); and what the designer permits the user to do that the designer did not specifically anticipate (appropriation provisions). The template reads in the first person, and it reads roughly like this:

"Here is my understanding of who you are, here is what I have learned you want or need to do, here is the system I have built for you, and here is how you can -- or should -- use it to achieve your goals."

This template is the reason SEJA's design intent is written the way it is. The canonical metacommunication message lives in §11 of product-design/product-design-as-intended.md, and it is voiced in the first person, designer "I" to user "you." That is not a house style. The first person carries the moral weight of the template. When the message says "I designed a postpone shortcut for you because I have learned you tend to over-schedule," a person is committing, in public, to a claim about the user and a choice made on their behalf. When the same functionality is described as "the system provides a postpone shortcut," no one is accountable for anything. The first-person phrasing rule is there to hold that accountability, and SEJA's constitution (principle T4) makes it non-negotiable: the canonical message must never be replaced by a passive-voice or third-person rewrite, because doing so severs the intentional authorship relationship between the designer and the user.

Barbosa et al. (2021), in their FAccT paper, extended this template into the Extended Metacommunication Template (EMT), which adds four lifecycle dimensions and a set of explicit ethical questions. SEJA operationalizes the EMT directly in its /design questionnaire and in §12 of the design intent file. That extension is substantial enough to warrant its own treatment; the next chapter, The Extended Metacommunication Template, walks through it.

Sign Systems in HCI

Within the same 2005 book, de Souza borrows from Peirce and Eco to sort interface signs into three classes (de Souza 2005, Ch. 4; and de Souza and Leitao 2009). Static signs are read in a single moment -- a label, a layout, an icon. Dynamic signs emerge only through interaction -- a state transition, a confirmation dialog, an animation that plays out over time. Metalinguistic signs are the signs that explain the other two -- help text, error messages, tooltips, the documentation you are reading now. These three classes are how the Semiotic Inspection Method decomposes an interface before reassembling the pieces into a single reading of the designer's message.

De Souza also picks up Eco's distinction between a signification system and a communication process (de Souza 2005, Ch. 2, citing Eco 1976). A signification system is the socially conventionalized pool of expression-and-content pairs a community has available -- the shared vocabulary. A communication process is what someone does when they reach into that pool and assemble an intentional message from it. The two are related but not identical, and the gap between the shared vocabulary and any particular use of it is exactly where invention lives.

SEJA's marker system is a designed signification system in precisely this sense. The lifecycle markers -- STATUS, ESTABLISHED, CHANGELOG_APPEND, and the wider set the harness's write path supports -- form a small, conventionalized vocabulary for lifecycle events. STATUS names where an item sits on its journey from proposed to implemented to established to superseded. ESTABLISHED is the stamp that records a human promotion. CHANGELOG_APPEND is the append-only channel for audit entries. Each marker has a fixed shape (enforced by apply_marker.py) and a fixed place (writes outside the allowed pattern are rejected by check_human_markers_only.py). Together they let you and the agent talk about where design intent has moved without either party having to rewrite prose every time something changes. That is a sign system in the semiotic sense, deliberately kept small. The full grammar is treated in the Pipeline Reference and the marker how-to; here the point is only that the harness treats its own conventions as signs, not as arbitrary formatting rules.

Communicability and Communication Breakdowns

Semiotic engineering supplies its own quality criterion. Communicability (de Souza 2005, Ch. 4; de Souza and Leitao 2009) is the measure of how well the designer's deputy succeeds at conveying the full metacommunication message -- not merely whether the user can complete a task, but whether the user grasps the designer's intent, the range of what the system affords, and why it behaves as it does. A system can be usable and still communicate poorly, if the user gets the job done while badly misreading what the designer meant.

SEJA reaches communicability mostly from the emission side -- the side where the designer composes the message -- rather than the reception side, where a user's understanding is observed directly. This is an honest structural limit, not an oversight: SEJA is a harness for producing software, not an analytical instrument for inspecting a running interface against real user behavior. Its contribution is to keep the designer's side of the message coherent and traceable -- through the design specs, the drift detection between intent and code, and the review perspectives -- so that whatever the user eventually reads is at least a faithful rendering of what the designer decided to say. What SEJA cannot do, and does not claim to do, is confirm that the user received the message as intended. That would require user-observation methods the harness does not carry.

What We Are Uncertain About

It would be dishonest to present these correspondences as settled. Two are genuinely shaky. The first is the first-person phrasing rule. It works well for a solo designer and small teams, but we do not know whether the first-person voice survives a ten-person design team without diluting into committee prose. The EMT's team-consensus process (treated in the next chapter) is an attempt to solve exactly this, and we have only run it in small collaborations. Somewhere there is a team size at which "I designed this for you" starts to read as performative, and we do not yet know where that ceiling is.

The second is the sign-class taxonomy. SEJA does not carry a first-class classification of interface signs into static, dynamic, and metalinguistic categories the way the Semiotic Inspection Method does; the taxonomy surfaces only inside the semiotic-inspection critique mode, not as a pervasive concept in the design specs. This is the single sharpest gap between SEJA and the semiotic-engineering literature. We think the gap is honest rather than accidental -- SEJA produces interfaces rather than inspecting them, and the sign-class taxonomy is fundamentally an inspection tool -- but a reader coming from the literature should know it is a gap and not mistake the harness for a complete operationalization of the theory.

What This Means for SEJA Users

If you take one idea from this chapter into your daily use of SEJA, make it this: the design-intent file is not project-management overhead, it is your message to your users, written down so it can survive contact with an AI agent. When /design asks you to state, in the first person, who your user is and what you have built for them, it is asking you to compose the metacommunication template. When the harness refuses to let the agent overwrite that message and permits only marker writes, it is protecting the designer's deputy from being quietly reauthored by the model. When /explain drift compares your intent against your code, it is checking communicability -- asking whether the message you meant to send is still the message the system actually sends.

Every one of those mechanisms is downstream of a single commitment: that the software you build with SEJA remains your message, accountable to you, even when a capable agent does much of the typing. The chapters that follow trace that commitment through the rest of the harness. The next one takes up the ethical extension of the template; from there we move to reflective practice, to the governance-and-epistemics thesis that unifies the design, to how the agent itself reads the harness, and finally to the concrete design trade-offs the harness makes.
