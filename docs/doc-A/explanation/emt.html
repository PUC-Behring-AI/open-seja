The Extended Metacommunication Template

The previous chapter introduced de Souza's metacommunication template: the first-person message in which a designer states who the user is, what they have learned the user needs, what they built, and how it may be used. That template was designed for a single designer sending a message about a finished system. Barbosa, Barbosa, de Souza, and Leitao (2021), in their FAccT paper, extended it in two directions that matter for a tool like SEJA. First, they made it lifecycle-aware: the message is not a one-shot artifact but something that is composed, revised, and re-examined across the whole development process. Second, they made it ethical: at each lifecycle stage, the designer is asked not only what they know and what they built, but what ethical questions their choices raise. The result is the Extended Metacommunication Template, or EMT, and it is the direct source of the design questionnaire SEJA runs.

Four Lifecycle Dimensions

The EMT organizes its guiding questions across four dimensions of the development lifecycle. Each dimension carries a set of base questions (what did I learn, what did I build, how should it be used) and, layered on top, explicit ethical questions. The four dimensions are:

Analysis
Understanding needs and defining requirements. The questions here ask what the designer knows and does not know about the user, about others affected by the system, and about the intended and anticipated contexts of use -- and what ethical questions those understandings raise. This is the dimension where epistemic honesty enters first: the template does not ask only "what do you know," it asks "what do you not know, and how do you know it."
Design
What the designer has designed, which of the user's goals it supports, in what contexts they intend it to be used, and -- critically -- for what purposes they do not want it used. The ethical layer asks which ethical principles influenced the design and how the system is aligned with them.
Prototyping, implementation, and formative evaluation
How the system was built to support the design vision, what was built in to prevent undesirable uses and consequences, what was built in to identify and remedy unanticipated negative effects, and what ethical scenarios were used to evaluate the system before release.
Continuous, post-deployment evaluation and monitoring
How much of the design vision is reflected in the system's actual use, what unanticipated uses have been made and by whom, what anticipated and unanticipated effects have resulted, and what ethical issues need to be handled through redesign, policy, or even decommissioning. This dimension is where the message stops being a prediction and becomes an observation.

The four dimensions are not phases you pass through and leave behind. They are lenses that stay open across the life of the system. The post-deployment dimension in particular is a standing invitation to revise everything upstream of it: what you observe in real use feeds back into what you now know (analysis) and what you should redesign.

The Ethical Questions

What distinguishes the EMT from the original template is that it treats ethics as a first-class part of the metacommunication message rather than as a separate compliance exercise. At each dimension, the designer is pressed on accountability, on the prevention of harm, on the purposes the system should not serve, and on the ethical principles that shaped the design. The move is deliberate: because the metacommunication message is the designer speaking in the first person, folding ethics into it means the designer must own their ethical choices in the same voice they use to own their design choices. There is no passive "the system ensures fairness"; there is "I designed this constraint because I believe...".

SEJA carries these questions verbatim in §12 of product-design-as-intended.md. The harness's own EMT answers make the pattern concrete. The primary ethical question SEJA identifies for itself is accountability: when AI-assisted software causes harm, who is responsible? SEJA's answer is that its governance model makes the designer's intent explicit and traceable, which supports accountability -- but it is candid about the limit. SEJA can enforce that the implementation matches the intent; it cannot enforce that the intent itself is ethical. A designer who writes an unethical metacommunication message gets a system that faithfully executes it. That candor is itself an EMT move: naming the ethical question you cannot resolve is part of answering it honestly.

Distributed We, Collective We, I

The EMT also names a team dynamic that the original single-designer template had no vocabulary for. In a real design team, there is no single "I" at the start. Barbosa et al. describe the process as "distributed we to collective we to I": each member of a design team first writes their own metacommunication message (the distributed we -- many separate voices), the team then negotiates a shared version (the collective we -- one agreed-upon reading), and finally converges on a single first-person voice that can speak to the user as one designer (the I).

This matters because the first-person template is only honest if the "I" it speaks in is a genuine, negotiated commitment rather than a fiction papering over unresolved team disagreement. The process from distributed to collective to singular is how a team earns the right to speak in the first person. SEJA operationalizes it in two places. The /design questionnaire structures the elicitation so that the answers converge on a single voiced message. And for genuinely contested decisions, the council-debate mode of /research --deep assembles a small group of named expert perspectives, surfaces their disagreements explicitly, and drives toward a synthesis -- an operationalization of the negotiation from many readings toward one. We are candid that this is only a partial fit: if the council never produces a genuine "I" at the end and instead preserves a plural voice, the correspondence is analogous rather than exact. The uncertainty section below returns to this.

Operationalizing "Who Is the We"

A subtle contribution of the EMT is that it forces the question of whose intent the metacommunication message expresses. In SEJA's own design intent, the "I" is the harness author speaking to the SEJA user; the "you" is a software designer or developer who subscribes to semiotic engineering values and takes a responsible epistemic posture toward their own users. But the SEJA user, in turn, becomes an "I" speaking to their users when they compose their own project's metacommunication message. The template nests: SEJA's message to you models the message you are meant to write to the people who use what you build.

This nesting is why SEJA extends the EMT beyond a one-shot artifact. The /design questionnaire does not merely capture the four dimensions once and file them away. Because the design intent file is continuously compared against the as-coded state by /explain drift, and because /reflect can revisit it against actual use, the EMT becomes a standing instrument that tracks how much of the design vision is reflected in the running system -- which is precisely the question the post-deployment dimension asks. The EMT in SEJA is not a form you fill in; it is a message you keep answering as your understanding evolves.

What We Are Uncertain About

The strongest reservation concerns the "distributed we to collective we to I" process. SEJA operationalizes it through the questionnaire and the council-debate, but the operationalization is only as good as the synthesis it produces. The council-debate reliably produces plural readings; what is less certain is that its final synthesis does justice to the disagreement rather than collapsing it into a single tidy recommendation because recommendations are easier to act on. If the council keeps a plural voice at the end, or if it flattens genuine tension prematurely, then the mapping to the EMT's consensus process is partial at best. This is a place where SEJA's fit to the theory is aspirational rather than demonstrated.

A second reservation concerns the ethical questions themselves. The EMT only earns its ethical claim if designers actually engage the ethical sub-questions rather than treating them as boilerplate to click past. A questionnaire can carry an ethical question faithfully and still fail if the answer is rote. SEJA cannot enforce genuine ethical reflection any more than it can enforce genuine design intent; it can only make the questions unavoidable and the answers visible. Whether that is enough is an empirical question we cannot yet answer from a single-designer usage history.

What This Means for SEJA Users

When /design walks you through §12, it is not collecting metadata. It is asking you to compose the ethical, lifecycle- aware message the EMT describes -- to state not just what you built but what you do not yet know, what you do not want the system used for, and what ethical questions your choices raise. The "what I don't yet know" answers are not admissions of weakness; they are the epistemic honesty the whole template is built to protect. And because the design intent file is revisited across the lifecycle, you are meant to come back to these answers as real use teaches you things you could not have known at the start. The next chapter takes up the tradition that makes that revisiting a discipline rather than an afterthought: reflective practice.
