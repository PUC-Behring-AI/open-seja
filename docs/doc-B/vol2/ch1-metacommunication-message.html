The Metacommunication Message

At the apex of the SEJA design layer sits a single artifact: your metacommunication message. It is a first-person statement, written by you the designer and addressed to the person who will use what you build -- and, in a SEJA project, delivered through the agent that builds it. Everything else in the design layer -- the constitution, the design-intent files, the marker system, the UX research artifacts -- exists to keep the software accountable to this one message. This chapter explains what the metacommunication message is and why it holds the highest authority, walks you through writing one with the Extended Metacommunication Template as /design actually presents it, states the first-person phrasing rule and why it matters, and closes with a worked example.

What the Metacommunication Message Is

SEJA is grounded in Semiotic Engineering, a theory of human-computer interaction developed by Clarisse Sieckenius de Souza (The Semiotic Engineering of Human-Computer Interaction, de Souza 2005). Its central claim reframes what a piece of software is: not a tool that happens to have an interface, but a one-shot message from the designer to the user. Every screen, default, error text, and affordance is part of a single communicative act in which you tell the user who you think they are, what you have understood they want to do, and how you have designed the system to help them do it. Because you are not present at interaction time, this message must carry itself -- it speaks on your behalf when you are not in the room.

The metacommunication message is the explicit, distilled form of that designer-to-user message. Semiotic Engineering summarizes its content as: "Here is my understanding of who you are, what I've learned you want or need to do, in which preferred ways, and why. This is the system that I have therefore designed for you, and this is the way you can or should use it in order to fulfill a range of purposes that fall within this vision." In most software this message is implicit -- it is scattered across the UI and never written down. SEJA's move is to make it explicit: a structured artifact you author directly, recorded in product-design/product-design-as-intended.md (its global form lives in section 11, "Global Metacommunication Vision").

Delivered Through the Agent

A SEJA project adds one twist to the classic picture. In ordinary software the message travels from designer to user through the finished interface. In a SEJA project it travels through a second party first: the agent that plans and implements the system reads your metacommunication message and is bound by it. Making the message explicit is what lets it be loaded into every agent context, validated against what was actually built, and cited in the rationale of every plan. The agent becomes a faithful relay of your intent rather than an author of intent in its own right.

Why It Sits at the Apex

The metacommunication message is the highest-authority artifact in the design layer. Every other design and implementation decision is ultimately accountable to it. This is not a stylistic claim -- it is operational:

  • It grounds the design-intent files. The per-feature intentions and the guiding-question answers that fill product-design/product-design-as-intended.md all trace back to the global vision expressed in the message.
  • It is what drift is measured against. When /critique or /explain drift compares as-coded against as-intended, the metacommunication message is the anchor: has the system drifted away from what you said you were building for whom?
  • It is the top of the accountability chain. The constitution (see Chapter 2) names the Designer/Agent boundary as SEJA's core governance invariant precisely so that the metacommunication message stays authoritative -- if the agent could freely overwrite design intent, the message would cease to mean anything.

Tip: "Authoritative but not immutable." The apex position does not mean the message is frozen. SEJA's epistemic posture expects you to revisit it as your understanding of your users evolves. What the apex position does mean is that no plan or implementation may silently contradict it -- a change to the message is a design act, made by you, not a side effect of coding.

Writing One: The Extended Metacommunication Template

You write your metacommunication message inside /design. There are two moments where /design captures it, and a deeper questionnaire -- the Extended Metacommunication Template (EMT) -- that expands the short message into a structured design rationale. This section walks through all three as they actually appear in the skill.

Step 1: The short message (Section 0)

The very first thing /design's questionnaire asks (Section 0, metacomm-message, question 0.1) is your metacommunication message -- an optional 1–3 sentence statement from you (the designer) speaking directly to your future user. It is asked first for a reason: an agent filling in the rest of the questionnaire will extract your project name, description, target user type, and primary use case from this answer, then offer them as suggested defaults for later questions. Answering it well up front reduces how much of the questionnaire you have to fill from scratch.

The template's own worked example for question 0.1 reads: "I know you are a busy researcher who needs to track your experiments across projects. Therefore, I have designed a lightweight experiment tracking platform that lets you log runs, compare results, and share findings with your team."

Step 2: The fallback (Final Step)

Section 0 is optional. If you skip it -- and did not already supply a message at invocation -- /design returns to it as the Final Step of the questionnaire, after every other section is answered. This is deliberate: by then the agent has enough material to compose a recommended message for you, inferred from your earlier answers, and present it. You then choose one of three responses:

Accept
The message is recorded as-is. The agent appends a note recording that it was generated by the design agent during the session, so the provenance is never lost.
Edit
You modify the message; the agent records your edited version verbatim. No generation note is appended -- once you touch it, it is your words.
Reject and skip
No metacommunication message is recorded; the field is left blank.

If you supplied a message at invocation (in the initial prompt, in a spec file, or via a --framing metacomm brief), the agent reuses it verbatim as the Section 0.1 answer and skips the Final Step entirely.

Step 3: Deepen it with the EMT guiding questions (Section 12)

The short message states the design vision; the Extended Metacommunication Template makes you argue for it. The EMT is a set of guiding questions organized by the four stages of the design lifecycle, and its answers are recorded in section 12 of product-design/product-design-as-intended.md. Its defining feature is that it asks not only what you know but what you do not yet know -- the epistemic humility that runs through SEJA. The four stages are:

Stage Focus Representative guiding questions
1. Analysis Understanding needs; defining requirements What do I know or not know about you, and how? About affected others? About the intended and other anticipated contexts of use? What ethical questions does what I have learned raise?
2. Design What I designed and why What have I designed for you? Which of your goals does it support? In what contexts do I intend or accept you will use it? How should you use it? For what purposes do I not want you to use it? What ethical principles influenced my decisions?
3. Prototyping, implementation & formative evaluation How the build supports the vision How have I built the system to support my design vision? What have I built in to prevent undesirable uses and consequences, and to identify and remedy unanticipated negative effects? What ethical scenarios did I use to evaluate it?
4. Continuous, post-deployment evaluation & monitoring Living with the system after release How will I keep learning about how you actually use the system, and how will I fold that learning back into the design intent?

You do not have to answer every EMT question in one sitting, and the "I don't yet know" answers are first-class -- leaving one honestly unresolved is preferable to inventing a confident answer. The EMT is covered in theoretical depth in Vol.5; see Vol. 5, Chapter 3.

Tip: If your project already has design intent and you only want to revisit the message, you do not need to re-run the whole questionnaire. Invoke /design update metacomm -- /design jumps straight to the metacommunication update flow and leaves your other sections untouched.

The "I / You" Phrasing Rule

Every metacommunication message -- in templates, project files, plans, briefs, per-feature logs, and EMT answers -- must use first-person "I" for the designer and second-person "you" for the user. This is a non-negotiable rule in the harness. You never write "the designer", "the system", or "the user"; you never fall back on passive voice or the imperative mood.

The difference is not cosmetic. Compare:

Do write (I / you) Do not write
"I designed a postpone shortcut for you because I know you tend to over-schedule." "The designer provides a postpone shortcut." (third-person, designer erased)
"I want you to be able to reschedule a task in one keystroke, because I've learned you work under time pressure." "Enforce privacy with minimal friction." (imperative; no designer, no user, no why)
"I know you are a busy researcher, so I have designed a lightweight tracker for you." "The system tracks experiments for users." (passive; the communicative act disappears)

Why the rule matters: Semiotic Engineering treats the designer as a communicator who is personally present in the artifact. The "I / you" phrasing keeps a live person on both ends of the message -- an "I" who takes responsibility for the design and a "you" whose goals it answers to. Third-person and passive voice quietly delete one or both. "The system does X" hides the fact that you decided X; the imperative "do X" gives an instruction with no reason and no addressee. Because SEJA's whole value depends on design intent being authored by a named human and answerable to a named user, the harness enforces the grammar that keeps both visible.

A companion constraint travels with the phrasing rule: the verbatim rule. When you provide a metacommunication message as input, the agent must record it exactly as written, without summarization or editing. Your precise wording carries intentional nuance, and paraphrasing distorts design intent. Between the two rules, the message stays yours: your grammar, your words.

A Worked Example

The following is an illustrative example for a small hypothetical project -- a neighbourhood tool-lending library app. It is not a real SEJA project; it exists only to show the shape of a short "I want you to…" message that obeys the phrasing rule.

Example metacommunication message — "Borrow Bin" (hypothetical)

I know you are a neighbour who owns a few tools you rarely use and occasionally needs one you don't own, and that you'd rather lend to people nearby than let things gather dust. I want you to be able to list a tool in under a minute, find a tool someone near you is offering, and arrange a hand-off without exchanging phone numbers or money — so I have designed Borrow Bin to keep every listing local, every request one tap, and every lender's address hidden until you both agree to meet. I don't yet know how you prefer to handle a tool that comes back broken, so for now I've left that to a simple message between you and the borrower rather than pretending I have a fair rule.

Notice what the example does. It opens with who you are ("I know you are a neighbour who…"), states what I want you to do in concrete terms ("list a tool in under a minute…"), says what I therefore designed and why ("so I have designed Borrow Bin to…"), and -- in the SEJA spirit -- names one thing I don't yet know rather than papering over it. Every clause keeps an "I" and a "you" in view; there is no "the system", no passive voice, no bare imperative.

A message this size is enough to seed Section 0 of /design: an agent could extract the project name ("Borrow Bin"), a description (a local tool-lending app), the target user (a neighbour with tools to share), and the primary use case (listing and borrowing tools locally) as suggested defaults for the rest of the questionnaire.
