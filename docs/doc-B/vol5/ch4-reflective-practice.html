Reflective Practice

SEJA is a harness for a workflow, but it is also a theory of how good professional work happens. That theory has a name and a lineage: Donald Schön's reflective practitioner. When SEJA asks you to pause at an AskUserQuestion decision point, when it logs a Q&A transcript alongside each artifact, and when it offers /reflect at the end of a workflow, it is enacting a particular claim about expertise: that competent practitioners do not merely apply rules, but continuously converse with the situation in front of them, reshaping their understanding as they go. This chapter sets out Schön's account precisely, takes seriously the two strongest critiques it has attracted, and then examines how — and how faithfully — SEJA operationalizes each register of reflection. It ends where honesty requires it to: with the open question of whether an AI agent can reflect at all, or whether it merely scaffolds reflection for the human designer who remains the practitioner.

Schön's Registers of Reflection

In The Reflective Practitioner (1983), Donald Schön attacked what he called technical rationality: the dominant model of professional knowledge in which practice is the application of scientific theory to instrumental problems. Technical rationality assumes that problems arrive pre-formed and that expertise consists in selecting the correct technique. Schön observed that real practice looks nothing like this. The problems professionals face are messy, indeterminate, and value-laden; the hard part is not solving the problem but setting it — framing an ambiguous, troubling situation as a problem that can be worked on at all. Competent practitioners handle this indeterminacy through what Schön called an artistry that resists reduction to rules. His project was to give that artistry an epistemology, and the instrument he built for it was the vocabulary of reflection.

Schön distinguished several registers. Two are central, and a third is often conflated with them but should be kept distinct.

Reflection-in-action

Reflection-in-action is thinking that happens while the action is still underway, in the present tense of doing, and that reshapes the action before it is complete. A jazz musician who adjusts a phrase mid-improvisation to fit what the other players just did; a physician who, sensing that a case does not match the textbook, re-reads the symptoms on the fly; a designer who, watching a sketch fail to say what it was meant to say, redraws it differently — each is reflecting in action. Schön's phrase for this is a reflective conversation with the situation: the practitioner makes a move, the situation “talks back” with unexpected consequences, and the practitioner responds to that back-talk by reframing. Crucially, reflection-in-action is bounded by what Schön called the action-present — the stretch of time in which intervention can still make a difference to the outcome. It is not detached contemplation; it is inseparable from the doing.

Reflection-on-action

Reflection-on-action is the retrospective counterpart: thinking back over an episode after it is finished, in order to understand what happened, why a move worked or failed, and what the situation revealed about one's own tacit knowing. It is deliberate, past-tense, and separated from the pressure of the action-present. Where reflection-in-action is the pilot adjusting the controls, reflection-on-action is the debrief. It is the register most legible to writing, because it can be recorded, revisited, and shared — and it is therefore the register that documentation and logging most naturally serve.

Reflection-on-practice

Schön's later work (and the reception that grew around it) extends the frame to reflection-on-practice: reflection not on a single episode but on the pattern of one's practice over time — recurring habits, characteristic framings, the moves one reaches for by default. This is a broader and slower loop than reflection-on-action. Where reflection-on-action asks “what happened in this case?”, reflection-on-practice asks “what kind of practitioner do these cases, taken together, show me to be?” It is the register of professional identity and long-run growth, and — as this chapter will show — SEJA deliberately provides it a distinct lens rather than folding it into the per-episode retrospective.

Tip: A useful way to keep the three registers apart is by their time-relation to the action. Reflection-in-action runs during (present tense, inside the action-present); reflection-on-action runs after a single episode (past tense, one case); reflection-on-practice runs across many episodes (past tense, a pattern). SEJA maps a distinct mechanism onto each — see § How SEJA Operationalizes Each Register.

Two Counter-Readings

Schön's framework became one of the most cited accounts of professional learning of the late twentieth century, which is exactly why it deserves to be read against its strongest critics rather than adopted uncritically. Two critiques matter for SEJA, because each identifies a way the framework can mislead if applied naively — and SEJA is an application.

Eraut 1995: the “Schön Shock”

Michael Eraut's “Schön Shock” (1995) is the most careful conceptual objection. Eraut argues that Schön's central term, reflection-in-action, is dangerously ambiguous on the question of time. When exactly is the “action-present”? In a fast-moving clinical or design situation, the window in which one could genuinely reflect — stop, reconsider the frame, and alter course — may be so short that what actually occurs is rapid, intuitive, largely tacit judgment rather than anything we would recognize as reflection. Eraut's worry is that Schön conflates two different things: (1) fast, automatic professional judgment under time pressure, and (2) genuine deliberative reflection, which needs a pause that the action-present often does not afford. Squeeze the time available and “reflection-in-action” either collapses into intuition or smuggles in a covert interruption of the action. Eraut also charges that Schön's celebrated case studies are reconstructed retrospectively, so we cannot actually tell whether the reflection happened in the action or was narrated onto it afterward. The upshot for any system that claims to support reflection-in-action: be honest about whether you are supporting real in-flight reflection or merely inserting a decision pause — and be sure the pause is affordable.

Boud, Keogh, and Walker 1985: the emotional dimension

Where Eraut presses on time, David Boud and colleagues press on affect. In Reflection: Turning Experience into Learning (1985), Boud, Keogh, and Walker argue that Schön's account is too cognitivist — it treats reflection as an intellectual operation on a situation and largely omits the feelings that accompany experience. Their model of reflection has three phases, and the middle one is explicitly emotional: returning to and re-evaluating the experience requires attending to feelings — using positive feelings and, critically, removing obstructing feelings such as the discomfort, defensiveness, or frustration that can block honest re-examination. On this reading, reflection that suppresses or ignores affect is impoverished; the emotional charge of an episode is not noise to be filtered out but data about what mattered and where the practitioner is stuck. For a harness, this is a pointed critique: a reflection mechanism that captures only decisions and outcomes, and never the practitioner's felt sense of the work, is capturing the thinner half of what Boud et al. mean by reflection.

These are not footnotes to be filed and forgotten. Read together, the two critiques set a bar. Eraut says: do not claim in-action reflection where you have only a pause or an intuition. Boud et al. say: do not claim reflection at all if you have stripped out the affect. SEJA should be measured against both bars, not exempted from them.

How SEJA Operationalizes Each Register

SEJA does not treat “reflection” as a single feature. It maps a distinct mechanism onto each of Schön's three registers, and — as the following makes explicit — the mapping is exact in one case, approximate in another, and openly named as Schön in the third.

Register Time-relation SEJA mechanism
Reflection-in-action During the workflow AskUserQuestion decision points
Reflection-on-action After a single episode Post-skill Q&A logging (post-skill step 3)
Reflection-on-practice Across many episodes /reflect (product vs. practice lens; --deep mode)

Reflection-in-action via AskUserQuestion decision points

At points where a skill faces a genuine fork — whether to auto-run /document now or defer it, whether to apply STATUS markers immediately or file them for later review, which artifacts to bring into a reflection — SEJA surfaces an AskUserQuestion decision point rather than choosing silently. Each option carries a “Recommended when / NOT recommended when” rationale, so the choice is presented as a framing problem, not a menu. This is the harness's attempt at reflection-in-action: the workflow is still underway, the action-present is open, and the pause invites the designer to reconsider the frame before the move is committed. The AskUserQuestion pattern is used across the lifecycle — for example, in post-skill's documentation auto-run (2b), its DONE-marker proposal (2e), and its next-step suggestions (step 12), all defined in .claude/skills/post-skill/SKILL.md, and in the /reflect skill's own scope and lens prompts in .claude/skills/reflect/SKILL.md.

Eraut's critique lands squarely here, and it is worth conceding rather than deflecting. What an AskUserQuestion decision point actually provides is a structured pause — an interruption of the flow at which judgment is invited. Whether that rises to Schön's reflection-in-action depends on whether the designer uses the pause to re-frame or merely to click through. The harness can afford the pause and can load it with rationale; it cannot guarantee the reflection. SEJA arguably over-claims if it calls every decision point “reflection-in-action,” and the honest framing is Eraut's: these are affordances for in-flight judgment, and the pause is designed to be cheap enough to be affordable without so diluting it that it becomes a rubber-stamp.

Reflection-on-action via post-skill Q&A logging

After every skill completes, post-skill step 3 captures the Q&A of the session — the brief and the numbered exchanges that produced the artifact. This is verified against the live .claude/skills/post-skill/SKILL.md, whose step 3 defines three branches evaluated in order:

Branch A — skip
When the parent passed skip_qa_log: true. Used only when the skill's primary output is a Q&A transcript (currently /research, which embeds its own ## Q&A log).
Branch B — inline in the artifact file
For plan-, implement-, roadmap-, and check- artifacts, the Q&A is appended directly to the artifact under a ## Q&A Log section, with numbered ### Q1 / ### A1 exchanges continuing from the last entry.
Branch C — companion file
For all other artifact types (advisory, communication, onboarding, …), a sibling <prefix><id>-qa-<slug>.md file is written next to the parent artifact.

In every branch, the effect is the same: the episode is written down in the past tense, after the action is complete, in a form that can be revisited. That is precisely reflection-on-action's medium. The logging is not itself reflection — a transcript is not an insight — but it is the durable trace that later reflection reads from. For the mechanics of where step 3 sits in the pipeline, see Vol. 3, Ch. 1: The Lifecycle Pipeline.

Here Boud et al.'s critique bites. Post-skill Q&A logging captures questions, decisions, and outcomes — the cognitive residue of the episode — but it does not capture the designer's feelings about the work: the frustration at a stuck loop, the satisfaction of a clean cut, the unease that a shortcut was taken. Boud, Keogh, and Walker would say this is reflection with its emotional phase amputated. The one place SEJA does make room for felt experience is the conversational /reflect flow, which records the designer's own words verbatim — and there, affect can enter through the door of free text. The logging layer itself, though, is cognitivist by design, and that is a real limitation, not a rounding error.

Reflection-on-practice via /reflect

The /reflect skill is SEJA's dedicated instrument for the slower loop. Verified against the live .claude/skills/reflect/SKILL.md, its default conversational flow first asks the designer, via AskUserQuestion, to pick a lens — and the skill's own option text names Schön explicitly:

Product lens
“What I built as a thing in the world: does it do what I intended? what did it reveal?” Oriented to the artifact.
Practice lens
“How I worked as a practitioner (Schön's reflection-on-practice): what patterns emerge in how I designed and built this?” Oriented to the practitioner. The skill's own copy cites Schön here by name.
Both
Product and practice in sequence, when the designer is uncertain which lens fits or wants full coverage.

The lens then selects the reflection question the skill asks in plain text (never as multiple choice — the skill's rule is that reflection cannot be a menu): the product question asks what stands out about what you built; the practice question asks what stands out about how you worked and what you would do differently. The designer's answer is recorded verbatim in a reflection report under ${REFLECTIONS_DIR}. A strictly non-prescriptive rule governs the output: the skill writes observations and the designer's own words, never “you should,” “consider changing X,” or “we recommend” — a constraint enforced by an automated test that scans the generated report for the forbidden phrasings. This verbatim, non-prescriptive stance is what lets affect survive into the record: because the skill does not paraphrase, the designer's frustration or doubt is preserved as written.

Beyond the default conversational flow, the live skill defines a --deep mode that is explicitly practice-oriented — its own documentation calls it “Schön's reflection-on-practice.” Given a scope keyword and a time window (--deep [scope] [--since 30d]), it reads telemetry and briefs across the window and produces an event-matrix visualization, a transition graph, and a past-tense “practice evolution” narrative — surfacing how you worked over time, not what you built. A third mode, --telemetry, is a legacy statistical-mining flow over telemetry.jsonl; it too is practice-oriented and stamps its report with Lens: practice. The mapping to Schön is therefore not a documentation gloss layered on after the fact — it is written into the skill's own option copy and mode descriptions.

Tip: The three /reflect modes span the reflective loop by tempo. The default conversational flow anchors on one or a few artifacts — closest to reflection-on-action. --deep and --telemetry widen the window to a period of work — squarely reflection-on-practice. Choosing a mode is choosing how long a loop to close.

Critical Counter-Reading: Where the Mapping Strains

It would be easy to present the three-register mapping as a tidy success. It is not tidy, and pretending otherwise would violate the spirit of the theory being applied. Three strains deserve naming.

The in-action register is the weakest fit. By Eraut's own standard, a decision point is a pause, and a pause is not yet reflection-in-action. Schön's in-action reflection is continuous, embedded, and often tacit — the practitioner rarely stops. SEJA's mechanism is discrete and explicit: it does stop, at pre-declared forks. This is arguably a category difference, not a faithful implementation. The defensible claim is narrower than “SEJA supports reflection-in-action”: SEJA inserts affordable, rationale-bearing pauses at which in-flight judgment becomes possible. Whether the designer reflects is outside the harness's reach.

The affective dimension is under-engineered. Boud et al.'s emotional phase is present in exactly one place — the verbatim free text of a /reflect answer — and absent everywhere else. Post-skill logging, decision-point rationale, telemetry, and the --deep practice narrative are all cognitive and behavioral; none asks how the work felt. A designer who never opens a conversational /reflect will leave no affective trace at all. SEJA under-engineers here, and the gap is structural rather than incidental.

Automation can crowd out reflection. There is a tension internal to the harness. The more SEJA logs, indexes, and reflects on the designer's behalf — generating event matrices, transition graphs, and practice narratives automatically — the more it risks substituting the appearance of reflection for the act. A transition graph is not an insight; a verbatim answer the designer never wrote is not their reflection. SEJA's non-prescriptive rule is a deliberate guardrail against exactly this: by refusing to tell the designer what to conclude, the harness keeps the interpretive work where Schön would insist it belongs — with the practitioner. But the guardrail is a policy, not a guarantee, and the risk of reflection-theater is real.

Open Question: Can an AI Agent Reflect?

The deepest question this chapter raises cannot be closed here, so it is named as open. Schön's reflective practitioner is a person — someone with a professional identity, a stake in the outcome, and a felt sense of the situation talking back. When SEJA “reflects,” who is the practitioner?

Two readings are available, and the harness's own design leans decisively toward the second.

Reading 1 — the agent reflects
On this view, the agent's mid-workflow reconsideration of a frame, its retrospective narration of an episode, and its pattern-mining over telemetry are themselves species of reflection — functionally, if not phenomenally. The objection is immediate: an agent has no action-present in Schön's sense (nothing is at stake for it), no professional identity to grow, and, on Boud et al.'s terms, no feelings to attend to. What looks like reflection-in-action may be, in Eraut's terms, fast pattern-matching narrated as reflection — the very conflation Eraut warned against, now instantiated in silicon.
Reading 2 — the agent scaffolds the designer's reflection
On this view — the one SEJA's mechanisms actually enact — the practitioner is always the human designer, and the harness is scaffolding: it opens the pause (decision points), preserves the trace (Q&A logging), and prompts the loop (/reflect), but the reflecting is done by the person who reads the rationale, revisits the transcript, and writes the verbatim answer. The strictly non-prescriptive rule, the verbatim capture, and the “this is your practice” framing of the practice lens all point this way: SEJA is built to hand reflection back to the designer, not to perform it for them.

The honest position is that SEJA is designed as Reading 2 and should be described as Reading 2. Its value is not that the agent reflects — it may not, in any sense Schön or Boud would accept — but that it lowers the cost of the human designer's reflection: it makes the pause affordable, the trace durable, and the retrospective prompt unavoidable. Whether a future agent with persistent memory, self-model, and something answering to stakes could cross into Reading 1 is a question this documentation leaves open, precisely because SEJA does not currently answer it and should not pretend to.
