The Extended Metacommunication Template

Semiotic engineering holds that software is a one-shot message from designers to users, and that this message can be paraphrased in a single sentence: "Here is my understanding of who you are, what I have learned you want or need to do, in which preferred ways, and why. This is the system that I have therefore designed for you, and this is the way you can or should use it in order to fulfil a range of purposes that fall within this vision." That template — the metacommunication message — was formulated by de Souza (2005) for interactive systems in which the designer's deputy is the interface itself. Barbosa, de Souza, and colleagues (2021) extended it to a harder setting: systems that raise fairness, accountability, and transparency concerns, where the message must be answered not once but across a whole development lifecycle, and where the "designer" who authors it is rarely a single person. The result is the Extended Metacommunication Template (EMT). This chapter develops the EMT, shows how SEJA's /design questionnaire operationalizes it into a versioned artifact, and is honest about the one thing a static template cannot hold: the temporal record of what a designer learned, and unlearned, over time.

From the One-Shot Message to a Lifecycle Template

De Souza's original metacommunication message is a single utterance spoken through the completed artifact. It answers, in effect, "who did I design this for, and how do I intend it to be used?" It is expressed in the first and second person — "I" the designer, "you" the user — because the whole point of semiotic engineering is that an interface is a designer speaking, by proxy, to a person. The message is powerful precisely because it is compact: it forces the designer to commit to a vision that the artifact will then either uphold or betray.

Barbosa et al. (2021) observed that for systems with significant ethical stakes, this single sentence is too coarse. A recommender that allocates opportunities, a triage tool that ranks people, an AI-assisted decision aid — each carries commitments that the one-shot message flattens. Their extension keeps the first-person voice but unfolds the single message across the phases of a development lifecycle, and at each phase attaches explicit ethical questions alongside the descriptive ones. The designer is asked not only "what did I design for you?" but "what ethical questions can be raised by what I have learned?" and "what have I built into the system to prevent undesirable uses and consequences?" The template thus becomes a reflective instrument: a structured place to record, per phase, what is known, what is not yet known, and what the designer is accountable for.

The Four Lifecycle Stages

SEJA reconciles the paper's framing into four ordered lifecycle stages. It is worth being precise here, because a common misreading treats the EMT as "four dimensions" of a single message. In SEJA's operationalization — verified against the live design template (see the operationalization section) — the EMT is four lifecycle stages, each carrying its own guiding questions, several of which are explicitly ethical:

Stage Focus Representative guiding questions
1. Analysis Understanding needs, defining requirements What do I know or don't know about you (and how)? About affected others? About the intended and other anticipated contexts of use? What ethical questions can be raised by what I have learned, and why?
2. Design What was designed, for whom, and why What have I designed for you? Which of your goals does it support? In what contexts do I intend or accept its use? For what purposes do I not want it used? What ethical principles influenced my decisions, and how is the system aligned with them?
3. Prototyping, implementation, and formative evaluation How the vision was built and guarded How have I built the system to support my design vision? What have I built in to prevent undesirable uses and consequences? To identify and remedy unanticipated negative effects? What ethical scenarios have I used to evaluate the system?
4. Continuous, post-deployment evaluation and monitoring What use actually reveals over time How much of my vision is reflected in actual use? What unanticipated uses have been made, by whom, and why? What effects have resulted, and who do they affect? What ethical issues need handling through redesign, policy, or even decommissioning?

Tip: The four stages map onto SEJA's own lifecycle. Analysis aligns with /research and the metacomm intake; Design with /design; Prototyping/implementation with /plan and /implement; and Continuous evaluation with /critique and /reflect. The EMT is not a form to fill once — it is the shape of the whole development arc, written in the first person.

The Articulation Process: Distributed We, Collective We, I

The subtlest contribution of Barbosa et al. (2021) is not a new question but a claim about who speaks. De Souza's original "I" is a convenient fiction: even for a modest interface, the designer's voice is really the settled voice of a team — product, engineering, research, legal. For an ethically loaded system, that fiction strains. The paper describes an articulation process by which the many actual authors of a system converge on a single accountable voice. It can be read as a movement through three positions:

The distributed we
The many contributors as they actually are: dispersed across roles, time zones, and disciplines, each holding a partial and sometimes conflicting understanding of who the users are and what the system should do. At this stage there is no single message — there are many, latent and uncoordinated.
The collective we
The contributors as they deliberately coordinate: negotiating a shared understanding, resolving conflicts, agreeing what the system is for and what it must not do. The EMT's ethical questions are the forcing function here — "for what purposes do I not want you to use the system?" cannot be answered without the collective actually deciding.
The designer's I
The single first-person voice in which the finished message is spoken. This "I" is a constructed speaker: an accountable position that the collective agrees to stand behind, so that the user is addressed by a coherent designer rather than a committee. The move from we to I is a deliberate assumption of responsibility, not a grammatical convenience.

This articulation matters for SEJA because the harness inserts a new participant into the relay. In an ordinary product, the collective's I speaks through the interface directly to the user. Under SEJA, the designer's I speaks first to an agent, which then acts on the design intent to build and govern the system the user eventually meets. The metacommunication is therefore a designer-to-agent-to-user relay: the designer authors intent, the agent reads it as its authoritative brief, and the artifacts the agent produces carry that intent onward. The EMT is what makes the first hop of that relay legible — it is the document the agent reads to know whose "I" it is deputizing for.

Note: The "distributed we → collective we → I" articulation is a theoretical frame from the paper. It does not appear as an explicit mechanism anywhere in the live harness — SEJA does not model the roster of contributors or track their convergence. What SEJA encodes is the output of the articulation: a single first-person design-intent artifact. Whether the "I" it records was genuinely articulated by a collective, or asserted by one person, is a discipline the harness cannot verify. See the critical counter-reading.

How /design Operationalizes the EMT

SEJA turns the EMT from a conceptual template into a concrete, versioned artifact through the /design skill. The claims in this section are verified against the live skill and template files, not against the public foundations prose (which lags): the design skill definition (.claude/skills/design/SKILL.md), the design questionnaire (.claude/references/template/questionnaire.md), and the design-intent template (.claude/references/template/product-design-as-intended.md).

The Intake: Section 0 and the First-Person Rule

The entry point to the EMT is the metacommunication message itself. The questionnaire's Section 0 asks the designer for an optional one- to three-sentence message spoken directly to future users, under a strict phrasing rule: use "I" (designer) and "you" (user), never the third person. This is the compact de Souza message, preserved intact. The questionnaire treats it as load-bearing: an agent filling in the form extracts the project's description, target user type, and primary use case from this message and offers them as defaults for later questions, so the metacommunication genuinely governs the rest of the design rather than sitting decoratively at the top.

The Expansion: Section 12 of the Design-Intent File

The compact message is then expanded into the full four-stage EMT in section 12 of product-design-as-intended.md, titled "Extended Metacommunication Template Guiding Questions." In the template, each guiding question is a placeholder token — for example {{EMT_ANALYSIS_WHAT_I_KNOW_OR_DONT_KNOW_ABOUT_YOU_AND_HOW}} and {{EMT_DESIGN_FOR_WHAT_PURPOSES_DO_I_NOT_WANT_YOU_TO_USE_THE_SYSTEM}} — that /design replaces with the designer's answers. The four stages appear in order: Analysis, Design, Prototyping, implementation, and formative evaluation, and Continuous, post-deployment evaluation and monitoring. Each stage's ethical questions are first-class placeholders, so the ethical reflection cannot be silently skipped — the template has a labelled slot waiting for it.

The Verification Pass

Operationalization is enforced, not merely offered. The design skill's Mode 1 verification pass (step 11) reads the generated product-design-as-intended.md and checks, among other things, that the section 12 EMT guiding questions are populated from the spec rather than left as placeholders, and that the metacommunication message matches the generated Global Vision. If gaps are found, the skill fills them and re-runs the check once. This closes the loop between the intake message and the expanded template: a project whose EMT slots were left blank fails the verification and is repaired before the design is considered done.

EMT element Where it lives in SEJA How it is written
Compact metacommunication message Questionnaire Section 0; Global Vision in the design-intent file Designer-authored, first person ("I"/"you")
Four-stage EMT guiding questions Section 12 of product-design-as-intended.md Designer answers substituted for {{EMT_...}} placeholders by /design
Ethical questions per stage Dedicated slots within Section 12 First-class placeholders; verification pass checks they are filled
Epistemic gaps ("what I don't yet know") Analysis-stage answers (e.g. question 1.1) Designer-authored; the template explicitly invites uncertainty

Tip: The Analysis stage's question 1.1 — "what do I know or don't know about you?" — is where SEJA's epistemic ambition lives. The template rewards a designer for recording ignorance, not hiding it. A design-intent file that says "I don't yet know my users' contexts of use" is more faithful to the EMT than one that pretends to complete knowledge.

Open Question: The Temporal Dimension in a Static Template

The EMT's fourth stage — continuous, post-deployment evaluation — is explicitly about time. It asks what actual use has revealed, what unanticipated uses have emerged, and what effects have accrued. But the artifact that holds these answers, product-design-as-intended.md, is a single current-state document. It records what the designer believes now. This creates a genuine tension: the template that is supposed to capture learning over time is, structurally, a snapshot.

Consider a designer who wrote, at the Analysis stage, "I don't yet know my users' contexts of use," and who, six months later, has learned a great deal from field observation. If they simply overwrite the Analysis answer with their new understanding, the record of what they once did not know is lost — and with it the honest trajectory the EMT was meant to preserve. The very epistemic humility the template rewards is erased by the act of updating it.

How SEJA Partially Addresses It

SEJA does not solve this cleanly, but it does not ignore it either. Several mechanisms carry temporal information around the static template:

  • An append-only changelog. The design-intent file carries a CHANGELOG section written through the marker path (apply_marker.py), so revisions leave a dated trail even when the prose above them is replaced.
  • Decision records. Consequential changes of intent are recorded as D-NNN entries with rationale and rejected alternatives, giving each shift of the "I" a durable, attributable history.
  • Reflection artifacts. The /reflect skill captures, at chosen moments, what was surprising and what is now known that was not before — a longitudinal record that lives beside, not inside, the template.
  • Discovered journeys. Field observations that contradict the designed vision accumulate in the UX research artifacts as append-only discovered journeys, so the gap between intended and actual use is recorded rather than reconciled away.

The honest position is that these are compensations, not a resolution. The EMT's fourth stage asks a longitudinal question of a point-in-time document, and SEJA answers it by scattering the temporal record across a changelog, a decision log, reflection files, and research artifacts. Reconstructing "what did the designer believe, and when?" requires reading all of them together. Whether the EMT should instead be versioned as a sequence of dated snapshots — a genuine timeline of design intent — remains an open question for the harness. It is recorded here as a limitation, not a solved problem.

Critical Counter-Reading

The EMT is a strong instrument, and it is worth naming precisely where it stops.

The template enforces form, not sincerity
SEJA's verification pass can confirm that the EMT slots are filled; it cannot confirm that they were filled honestly, or by a genuinely articulated collective. A designer can write a fluent, complete EMT that reflects no real deliberation — "accountability theatre," in the harness's own words. The template makes the absence of an answer visible, which is real value, but a present answer is not evidence of the reflection the EMT was meant to provoke.
The articulation process is assumed, not supported
The "distributed we → collective we → I" movement is the theoretical heart of the extension, yet the harness offers no tooling for it — no roster of contributors, no record of how conflicts were resolved, no trace of who dissented from the collective "I." For a solo designer this is harmless; for a team system with real fairness stakes, it means the single most ethically load-bearing step happens entirely outside the harness's view.
The relay adds a link the template does not model
The EMT was designed for a designer-to-user message. SEJA's relay inserts the agent between them, but the template has no slot for the agent's reading — no place to record where the agent may have misread the intent, or where its own inferences filled a gap the designer left open. The metacommunication is treated as if it passed through the agent transparently. In practice the agent is an interpreter, and interpreters distort.
Over-engineering for small projects
Four lifecycle stages with per-stage ethical questions is a heavy instrument for a weekend tool with no ethical stakes. The EMT was motivated by fairness-, accountability-, and transparency-sensitive systems; applied indiscriminately, it risks turning a proportionate reflection into ceremony. SEJA makes Section 0 optional, but the full section-12 expansion is generated regardless — a place where the harness may under-serve the designer of something small.

None of this diminishes the EMT's central achievement: it takes an idea — that a designer owes users an accountable account of what was built and why — and gives it a shape that a lifecycle can carry. SEJA's contribution is to make that shape a first-class, verified artifact rather than an aspiration. The gap between a filled template and a genuinely reflective one is exactly where the designer's irreducible responsibility lives.
