Sign Systems and Communicability

The previous chapter established the founding premise of semiotic engineering: software is a message from designers to users, sent once at design time and unfolded across every subsequent interaction. This chapter supplies the analytic machinery that makes the premise operational. If software is a message, then it is built from signs organized into sign systems, and its success can be judged by a single criterion -- communicability: the degree to which the designer's deputy succeeds in conveying, through those signs, who the users are thought to be, what they are thought to want, and how the artifact is meant to be used.

The chapter proceeds in four movements. First, it introduces de Souza's Semiotic Inspection Method (SIM) and its partition of interface signs into three classes -- metalinguistic, static, and dynamic. Second, it recovers Eco's foundational distinction between signification and communication, and explains why the distinction is indispensable for interface analysis. Third, it describes the SigniFYI Suite, the SIM-anchored family of methods that de Souza and colleagues assembled to capture and analyze metacommunication across a design's lifetime. Finally, it grounds all of this in SEJA itself, showing that the marker system is a designed signification system and that the review perspectives constitute a sign-class-aware inspection regime -- before closing with a critical counter-reading of where the sign-system analogy holds and where it is stretched when applied to an agent harness.

Signs, Sign Systems, and Communicability

A sign, in the tradition semiotic engineering draws on, is anything that stands to somebody for something in some respect. A label on a button, the shape of an icon, a colour that shifts from grey to red, a sentence in an error message -- each is a sign, because each is taken by an interpreter to mean more than its bare physical form. A sign system is a structured repertoire of such signs together with the conventions that govern how they combine and how they are to be read. Natural language is a sign system; so is a colour code, a menu grammar, or the vocabulary of states a form can occupy.

Semiotic engineering evaluates an interface not primarily for usability in the ergonomic sense but for communicability -- the property of an interactive artifact that lets its users readily and consistently reconstruct the designer's intended message about what the system is, whom it is for, and how it should be used. A highly usable interface can still have poor communicability: users may complete tasks by trial and error while never grasping why the system is shaped as it is, leaving them stranded the moment a task falls outside the well-worn path. Communicability is therefore about the transmission of design rationale, not merely task throughput.

Tip: Communicability is the emission-side counterpart to usability's reception-side concern. Where usability asks "can the user do the task?", communicability asks "does the user understand the designer's story about the task well enough to improvise when the task changes?"

The Semiotic Inspection Method and Its Three Sign Classes

De Souza and colleagues (2006) advance the Semiotic Inspection Method (SIM) as the inspection technique proper to semiotic engineering. SIM is an analyst-led method: an evaluator, acting as a knowledgeable advocate for the user rather than as a stand-in for any particular user, reconstructs the designer's deputy's message from the signs present in the interface and then judges whether that message is coherent enough to be received. The method's organizing move is to partition the interface's signs into three classes and to analyze each class independently before collating the results -- a discipline that keeps the analyst from smoothing over contradictions between what the interface says, what it shows, and what it does.

The three sign classes, defined

The three classes are distinguished by the kind of interpretation each sign invites. Precise definitions follow.

Metalinguistic signs
Signs that explicitly inform, illustrate, or explain other signs. They are the interface talking about its own signs and behaviours: help text, documentation, tooltips, onboarding and tutorial copy, error and validation messages, confirmation dialogs, empty-state prompts, and notifications. They are "meta" because their referents are other signs in the same interface. They carry the most direct trace of the designer's intent, but users frequently skip them, so a message lodged only in metalinguistic signs is a message at risk.
Static signs
Signs whose meaning can be interpreted independently of temporal or causal relations -- a single-moment snapshot of the interface. Layout, component structure, labels, icons, form fields, menu options, navigation hierarchy, button text, visual grouping, and typography are static signs. They express what the system is at rest: the vocabulary and grammar of the artifact frozen in one frame.
Dynamic signs
Signs bound to temporal and causal aspects of interaction -- meanings that emerge only through use. State transitions, conditional rendering, validation flows, loading and progress states, hover and focus feedback, progressive disclosure, animation triggers, and navigation transitions are dynamic signs. They express how the system behaves: what happens, in what order, and in response to what. Dynamic signs cannot be read from a screenshot; they must be enacted.

SIM analyzes each class in isolation, reconstructing a separate message from each -- conventionally message X from metalinguistic signs, Y from static signs, and Z from dynamic signs -- and only then collates the three. Collation is where communicability is judged. The analyst asks whether the three messages are consistent (do they tell the same story?), complete (is every part of the designer's intent carried by at least one class?), appropriately redundant (are critical messages reinforced across classes, rather than contradicting one another?), and well distributed (is the burden of meaning spread across classes, or dangerously concentrated in one -- for instance, all meaning in metalinguistic help that users never open?). Where the three voices agree, the interface tells one coherent story; where they diverge, the analyst has located a communicability breakdown.

Tip: SIM evaluates emission -- what the designer's deputy communicates. Its sibling method, the Communicability Evaluation Method (CEM; Prates, de Souza, & Barbosa, 2000), evaluates reception by observing breakdowns as real users encounter them. The two are complementary: SIM inspects the message as sent; CEM measures the message as received.

Signification vs. Communication (Eco)

Underneath SIM's three classes sits a distinction Eco draws in A Theory of Semiotics (1976) between two phenomena that ordinary language runs together. A signification system is a code -- a socially conventionalized correlation between expressions and contents that holds whether or not anyone is currently using it to say anything. The traffic-light code, in which red means stop, exists as a signification system even at three in the morning on an empty road. Communication, by contrast, is the actual process in which a sender exploits a signification system to produce a message that a receiver then interprets. Eco's thesis is that communication presupposes signification -- there is no communicating without a code to communicate with -- but the two are analytically separate: a code can exist unused, and a communicative act can misfire even when the code is sound.

This separation matters for interface analysis in three concrete ways.

  • It locates two distinct failure modes. An interface can fail because its underlying code is impoverished or contradictory (a signification defect -- the icons simply do not reliably correlate with any content), or because a well-formed code is deployed in a way that does not reach the user (a communication defect -- the right message exists but is buried where no one looks). SIM's collation step is precisely the search for both: mismatches between classes are often signification defects, while maldistribution is a communication defect.
  • It justifies analyzing the artifact without watching a user. Because the signification system is present in the artifact independently of any given communicative episode, an analyst can inspect it directly -- which is exactly what SIM does. The method reads the code off the interface rather than inferring it solely from observed behaviour.
  • It reframes the designer's task. The designer's job is not only to send a good message but to author the signification system through which every future message will be sent. In semiotic engineering the interface is the code and the message at once; the designer legislates the vocabulary users will be forced to interpret.

Tip: Eco's The Role of the Reader (1979) develops this into the apparatus of the model reader: a text encodes a reader whose competences it presupposes. An interface likewise encodes a model user. When the real user's competences diverge from the encoded model, the code is intact but communication breaks -- the theme Volume 5 Chapter 5 develops under plural readings.

The SigniFYI Suite

SIM inspects a finished artifact at a single point in time. But metacommunication is authored, revised, contested, and re-explained across a whole design process, and much of that authoring never reaches the shipped interface -- it lives in design documents, discussions, and the tacit reasoning of the team. To capture metacommunication as it is produced, not only as it is finally emitted, de Souza and colleagues (2016) assembled the SigniFYI Suite: a coordinated set of SIM-based tools for capturing, tracing, and analyzing metacommunication across the software lifecycle.

Described at a high level, the suite bundles complementary instruments around the SIM core. It provides means to capture the traces of a design process -- the artifacts, decisions, and discussions in which the designer's message takes shape; a manifestation lens that makes the intended metacommunication explicit and inspectable; and analytic apparatus for reading the resulting record through the semiotic-engineering frame, so that consistency, completeness, redundancy, and distribution can be assessed not just in the final product but along the path that produced it. The suite also carries forward the SigniFYIng Interaction quality dimensions -- consistency, completeness, redundancy, and distribution -- as the rubric against which reconstructed metacommunication is judged. The governing idea is that a design's communicability is better understood, and better repaired, when the message can be traced back through the decisions that formed it.

The SigniFYI Suite is a research instrument. SEJA does not implement the suite as such; it borrows the suite's central discipline -- treat the whole record of design intent as inspectable metacommunication -- and operationalizes it in the harness-specific ways described next.

How This Grounds SEJA

SEJA is a harness for designed communication, and the two constructs just introduced -- a signification system and a sign-class-aware inspection -- map onto concrete harness machinery.

The marker system as a designed signification system

SEJA's marker system is a deliberately authored signification system laid over the project's design-intent files. Its expression plane is a small, fixed vocabulary of HTML-comment markers written immediately before the heading, table row, or bullet they qualify; its content plane is the lifecycle state of the item so marked. Verified against .claude/references/general/shared-definitions.md, the vocabulary comprises: a STATUS marker whose current (SEJA 2.8.0+) form is a lowercase multi-value token driven by an explicit state machine (proposed → implemented → established → superseded, with regressions rejected); an ESTABLISHED stamp recording promotion; an INCORPORATED stamp; and the append operations CHANGELOG_APPEND and DECISION_APPEND. Legacy uppercase STATUS: IMPLEMENTED markers remain valid and are detected so that a promotion replaces rather than stacks.

What makes this a signification system in Eco's precise sense is that the correlation between expression and content is fixed by convention and enforced independently of any single act of use. The allowed transitions live in a registry (human_markers_registry.ALLOWED_MARKERS); the sole sanctioned write path is apply_marker.py; and post-skill step 6c runs check_human_markers_only.py to reject any prose mutation of these "Human (markers)" files, so the agent may write only well-formed markers and only after an explicit confirmation. The code is thus authored once and then governs every later message an agent tries to emit into those files -- exactly the relationship signification bears to communication. The marker system is treated at length in Volume 2. See The Marker System.

Review perspectives as sign-class-aware inspection

SEJA's inspection regime is realized in two layers, both verified against live harness files. The general one is the review perspectives framework (.claude/references/general/review-perspectives.md): a checklist of sixteen domain lenses -- eleven engineering (SEC, PERF, DB, API, ARCH, DX, I18N, TEST, OPS, COMPAT, DATA) and five design (UX, A11Y, VIS, RESP, MICRO) -- each carried at three depth tiers (Essential / Standard / Deep, priority-classified P0 through P4) and resolved with an Adopted / Deferred / N/A status. This is not itself SIM, and the chapter should not overstate it: it is a sign-class-aware inspection in the looser sense that several of these lenses are precisely where communicability is evaluated -- the UX and DX perspectives carry the emission/reception Communicability sub-sections.

The genuinely SIM-shaped inspection is narrower and explicit: the semiotic-inspector agent (.claude/agents/semiotic-inspector.md), invoked through /critique semiotic-inspection. This agent conducts a full SIM evaluation across the three sign classes exactly as defined above -- reading metalinguistic signs (help, errors, tooltips), static signs (layout, labels, structure), and dynamic signs (transitions, validation flows, loading states) independently; reconstructing messages X, Y, and Z; then collating them against the SigniFYIng Interaction dimensions of consistency, completeness, redundancy, and distribution to produce a communicability judgment. Its own documentation cites de Souza (2005), the SIM papers, and de Souza et al. (2016) for those dimensions -- so SEJA's sign-class inspection is not an analogy imposed from outside but a direct implementation of the method this chapter describes.

Tip: The division of labour is deliberate. The sixteen review perspectives give broad, deterministic coverage of engineering and design concerns on every change; the semiotic-inspector supplies the deep, dedicated three-class reading when communicability itself is the question.

Critical Counter-Reading

The sign-system frame earns its place in SEJA, but honesty requires marking where it is strong and where it is stretched when carried from human-computer interfaces to an agent harness.

Where the analogy is strongest

The marker system is a near-textbook signification system: a small, conventionalized, machine-enforced code whose expressions correlate reliably with contents (lifecycle states) and whose use is governed independently of any single act -- precisely Eco's picture. Likewise, the harness's user-facing documentation and skill outputs are genuine interface signs across all three SIM classes, so a SIM reading of, for example, a skill's help text (metalinguistic), its command grammar (static), and its multi-step prompting flow (dynamic) is well founded. Where SEJA presents surfaces that a human reads and acts on, the analogy is not an analogy at all -- it is the method's home ground.

Where the analogy is stretched

Three tensions deserve naming.

The receiver is partly a model, not a person
SIM was built for interfaces read by humans, and its analyst is an advocate for the user. In an agent harness a large fraction of the "signs" -- reference files, agent prompts, structured specs -- are authored to be read by a language model. Communicability then becomes partly a claim about how reliably a model reconstructs intent from text, which is an empirical property of the model, not only of the code. The theory frames the problem well but does not, by itself, tell us whether a given phrasing communicates to a given model. This is an open question, not a solved one.
Static/dynamic partition blurs for text-and-tools artifacts
SIM's static-versus-dynamic cut is crisp for a GUI, where a screenshot really does freeze the static signs. Much of SEJA is prose plus tool invocations, where the "temporal and causal" character of a sign is a matter of when the agent reads it and what it triggers, not of on-screen animation. The three classes remain a useful heuristic, but their boundaries are softer here, and an analyst can over-fit signs into classes the artifact does not naturally support -- a risk the semiotic-inspector's own scaffold questions (which explicitly ask whether signs are misclassified) are designed to check.
Enforcement can over-engineer the code
A machine-enforced signification system buys reliability at the cost of expressive range. The marker vocabulary is deliberately tiny, and its state machine forbids regressions; this is a feature for audit integrity but means legitimate design realities that do not fit the four states must be expressed elsewhere or not at all. Conversely, the sixteen review perspectives risk the opposite failure -- under-engineering communicability by treating it as one concern folded into UX/DX rather than a first-class axis, so that a change with subtle metacommunication drift can pass a standard review without ever triggering the dedicated semiotic-inspector. Whether communicability deserves its own always-on perspective, rather than an opt-in critique mode, is a live design question for the harness.

None of these tensions defeats the frame; they mark its edges. The sign-system analysis tells SEJA what to look for -- a coherent, well-distributed message carried by an authored code -- while leaving genuinely open how well that message lands on a machine reader and how much enforcement is the right amount.
